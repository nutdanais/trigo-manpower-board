The People chart follows the chip

Today the chart in People always shows Workload spread. This page shows what it could show for each chip (Workload, Leave, Nights, On-call, Alerts only), in three layouts to choose from. Every mockup is live: pick a chip, switch a view, then click a band, a day or a flag and the table filters.

What People does today

  • The chips only change the table: its columns, its sort and its filter. The chart above them never changes.
  • The chart sits above the chips, so it reads as the section's header, not as something a chip controls.
  • Two "who" switches. Permanent / On-call on the chart, Everyone / Permanent / On-call on the table. Clicking a band copies the chart's group into the table.
  • Chart-like lines sit between the chips and the table: the On-call chip's "days called in: median, never called in" and the Nights chip's "N people share the nights".
  • History already draws leave per day (with the Calendar) and on-call per day. People can reuse that drawing for the people in scope.
  • The data is already loaded. Who was on leave, who worked, who worked nights and who was on call each day all come from History's fetch. No new query and no database change.
  • One trap for On-call. For on-call people, "share called in" and "share worked" differ only by their leave, so a straight copy of the workload curve would look the same. The On-call chart divides by available days (working days less leave) and gives "never called in" its own bar.

What each chip shows

The same in all three layouts

The headline column is worked out from the sample data below. The layouts differ only in how the second view is reached.

The same rules for every chart

  • One scope. The Who switch (Everyone, Permanent, On-call) scopes the chart and the table together. The On-call chip sets it to On-call and hides it.
  • A headline line above each chart carries the numbers that sit above the table today.
  • Click to filter. A band, a day or a flag filters the table. A chip names the filter, with Clear. A day also offers "Open that day's board", the click History already has.
  • The app's colours. Blue for worked, orange for on-call, the four leave-type colours, amber above 20% and red above 30% leave share.
  • The table stays as it is: columns per chip, sorting, flags, a name opens the profile. Per-person figures stay behind the Overview History permission.
A

One chart per chip

Recommended

The chip picks the chart. Where a second view answers a different question it is one small switch in the chart header: Leave has Over time · Calendar · Spread (it opens on Over time), On-call has Spread · Per day. Workload, Nights and Alerts show one chart.

Good for
  • It is what you described: Leave shows leave over the period with a Calendar, On-call shows the call-in spread.
  • Least on screen. A switch only appears where it earns its place.
  • The day-by-day charts that History already has are not repeated for Workload.
Watch out
  • Leave opens on "when" while the other chips open on "who". That follows the question each chip asks.
  • Nights and Alerts have no time view.
B

Spread and Over time on every chip

The same switch on all five chips: Spread · Over time (Alerts says By flag · Over time), and Leave adds Calendar. Every chip can answer "who" and "when". Each opens on the view that fits its question.

Good for
  • One habit to learn. Every chip behaves the same way.
  • Any chip can answer "when did it happen", for example nights per day or the days people were on a long run.
Watch out
  • Workload's Over time repeats History's Deployed / day.
  • Ten charts to build, translate and test instead of seven.
  • The extra switch is on screen even where nobody will use it.
C

Both charts at once

Two panels side by side: who (the spread) on the left, when (per day, with Chart | Calendar for Leave) on the right. No switch to find. On a phone the panels stack.

Good for
  • Nothing is hidden behind a switch. A band and a day are both one click away.
  • Reads like a small dashboard for the chip.
Watch out
  • Each chart gets half the width. The calendar and the 11 on-call bands are tight.
  • It doubles the chart height on a phone, and the table starts further down.

Decisions for you

The first answer in each line is my recommendation.

  1. Layout. A A is the least to build and the closest to your description. B is more uniform, C shows everything at once. If you pick A and later want a time view for Nights, B's switch is a small step.
  2. Chips above the chart. Yes The chips move above the chart so they clearly drive both the chart and the table. The chart stops being the section's fixed header.
  3. One Who switch. Yes Everyone / Permanent / On-call scopes the chart and the table. With Everyone the spread is one combined curve. The alternative, two curves (permanent and on-call), is richer but busier.
  4. Leave over time, in People and in History. Keep both They use the same drawing. History shows leave for the board scope. People shows it for the Who scope, and a day click filters the table. History's day click still opens the board.
  5. On-call spread. Available days The bands use called in ÷ available days (working days less leave), with "Never" as its own red bar. Dividing by working days would repeat the Workload chart.
  6. Alerts chart. Bars by flag One bar per flag, split amber and red, and a click shows those people. The other choice is no chart for Alerts: the table stays the only view.

What it takes to build

  • Turn peopleSpread from ten fixed bands into one function that takes a list of bands (label, tone, count). Workload, Leave, Nights and On-call all use it.
  • Replace pplBand with one pick: a band, a day or a flag, shown as a chip with Clear.
  • Leave and on-call per day: call the History drawing code (historyLeaveView, historyLeaveCalendar, historyOncallView) with the people in scope. History's day records already hold leaveEmp, oncallEmp, workEmp and nightEmp per person.
  • The flag bars are the one new drawing.
  • Thai text for each new string, and tests in tests/e2e/overview-people.e2e.js.
  • No new query, no migration. A is about one pull request. B and C add the extra charts.
